Module 1: What Is Artificial Intelligence?

1.1 & 1.2 A Practical Definition

Artificial intelligence is computer software that learns from data to make decisions, predictions, or suggestions — without being explicitly programmed for every scenario.

The Keen Intern Analogy

Imagine Takudzwa, a keen intern at Parirenyatwa Hospital. He knows every medical textbook by heart, but has never touched a patient or felt a swollen spleen. He can suggest a diagnosis, but needs you to make the final call. AI is Takudzwa: brilliant with data, dependent on you for clinical decisions.

1.3 What AI Is Not

  • Not a robot: It is software on a phone or computer.
  • Not infallible: It can hallucinate and make confident errors.
  • Not replacing clinicians: A stethoscope didn't replace nurses; AI won't replace healthcare workers.

Module 3: The Four Types of AI in Healthcare

  • Natural Language Processing (NLP): Reads and writes text (e.g., ChatGPT drafting referral letters or Whisper converting voice to notes).
  • Computer Vision: Processes medical images (e.g., automated slide analysis for malaria parasites or diabetic retinopathy screening).
  • Predictive AI: Analyzes historical patterns to forecast events (e.g., sepsis deterioration alerts or malaria surge mapping).
  • Conversational AI: Interactive diagnostic dialogues (e.g., Ada Health symptom assessment).

Module 6: Ethics, Data Protection & Bias

6.1 Patient Confidentiality

Under Zimbabwe's Cyber and Data Protection Act (2021), never enter identifiable patient details (names, national ID, hospital numbers) into public AI platforms. Use strictly anonymized clinical scenarios.

6.2 Demographic Bias

Many diagnostic models were trained on European and North American populations. Clinicians must actively verify outputs for local demographics and darker skin presentations.
