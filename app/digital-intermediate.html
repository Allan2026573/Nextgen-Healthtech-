Module 1: Advanced EHR Workflow Optimization

1.1 Moving Beyond Basic Typing

Intermediate clinicians do not type the same paragraphs repeatedly. They optimize their time using built-in Electronic Health Record (EHR) features:

  • Auto-Text / Macros: Configuring short commands (e.g., typing `.asthma` to instantly generate a full asthma admission protocol template).
  • Customized Dashboards: Setting up your EHR home screen to immediately show pending lab results or critical alerts for your specific ward, rather than clicking through five menus.
  • Order Sets: Grouping common clinical orders (e.g., "Malaria Admission Set" containing FBC, RDT, and IV Artesunate) so they can be executed with a single click.

Module 2: Telemedicine and Remote Patient Monitoring

2.1 Synchronous vs. Asynchronous Telemedicine

Telemedicine bridges the gap between rural clinics and central specialists. It operates in two main modalities:

  • Synchronous (Real-Time): A live video or audio call between the clinician and the patient/specialist. Useful for urgent psychiatric evaluations or stroke assessments. The main limitation in rural Africa is the requirement for high-speed, stable internet.
  • Asynchronous (Store-and-Forward): A nurse at a rural clinic takes a high-resolution photo of a skin lesion, attaches it to the patient's digital file, and securely forwards it to a dermatologist in Harare. The specialist reviews it hours later. This is highly effective in areas with fluctuating internet connectivity.

The Informed Consent Rule

Before conducting any tele-consultation, the clinician must obtain and document explicit Informed Consent for telemedicine. The patient must understand that a video call has diagnostic limitations compared to a physical examination.

Module 3: Interoperability Basics (EHR & LIS)

3.1 When Systems Talk to Each Other

In a modern hospital, the ward's Electronic Health Record (EHR) does not operate in isolation. It must communicate with the Laboratory Information System (LIS).

When you click "Order Full Blood Count" on the ward, the EHR sends a digital message to the LIS. The lab machine analyzes the blood and automatically pushes the result back to the EHR on your ward tablet. This eliminates the need for nurses to physically walk to the lab to collect paper results.

3.2 The Invisible Language: HL7

The reason the ward computer can understand the laboratory computer is because they speak a common international language called HL7 (Health Level Seven). As an intermediate user, you don't need to code in HL7, but you must understand that if an interface "goes down," the lab results won't digitally cross over to the ward until IT restarts the HL7 connection.

Module 4: Clinical Coding and ICD-11

4.1 Why Clinicians Must Code

If you type "Heart Attack," another doctor types "Myocardial Infarction," and a third types "MI," a computer cannot easily group these together for national health reports. To fix this, the World Health Organization uses the ICD-11 (International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision).

4.2 The Impact of Accurate Coding

In digital systems, you select the diagnosis from a drop-down menu that assigns the official ICD-11 code automatically. Proper coding is vital for two reasons:

  1. Epidemiology: The Ministry of Health uses these codes to track national morbidity and mortality rates accurately in DHIS2.
  2. Medical Billing: Medical Aid societies (like CIMAS or PSMAS) will reject digital claims if the ICD-11 code does not match the provided treatment.

Module 5: Digital Audits and Data Protection

5.1 The Immutability of the Audit Trail

In paper records, a clinician might cross out a mistake with a pen. In a digital EHR, every single keystroke is permanently recorded in the Audit Trail. The system logs exactly who logged in, what time they opened a patient's file, and what data they changed. If a legal dispute arises, hospital administration can pull the audit trail to prove exactly what happened.

5.2 Zimbabwe Cyber and Data Protection Act

As an intermediate practitioner, you are responsible for enforcing data laws on your ward. Medical records are classified as Special Category Data. Snooping (opening the digital file of a patient you are not actively treating, such as a celebrity or a colleague) is not just a hospital policy violation; it is a prosecutable legal offense.

Module 6: Patient Portals and E-Prescribing

6.1 E-Prescriptions

Electronic Prescribing (e-Prescribing) eliminates handwritten prescription pads. The clinician selects the drug digitally, and the system instantly checks the patient's profile for drug-allergy interactions and drug-drug interactions. The script is then transmitted directly to the pharmacy queue, completely eliminating errors caused by illegible handwriting.

6.2 Patient Portals

Modern systems allow patients to log into a secure app on their phone to view their own lab results, appointment dates, and discharge instructions. While this empowers patients, clinicians must be mindful that patients read these notes. Documentation must be highly professional, avoiding derogatory abbreviations.

Module 7: First-Line System Troubleshooting

7.1 Solving Issues Before Calling IT

When a system stops working during a busy shift, an intermediate leader knows how to check the basics before raising a panic:

  • Check Physical Connections: Are the ethernet network cables and power cords securely plugged into the wall and the PC?
  • Browser Cache: If a web-based EHR is acting strangely, press Ctrl + Shift + R to perform a hard refresh and clear the cache.
  • Isolate the Problem: Is just your computer down, or is the whole ward down? If it is just your computer, a simple restart is the first line of defense.

The Intermediate Practitioner's Role

You are the bridge between clinical care and the IT department. By optimizing workflows, enforcing data privacy, and handling first-line troubleshooting, you ensure that technology accelerates patient care rather than hindering it.
