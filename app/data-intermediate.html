Module 1: Descriptive vs. Diagnostic Analytics

1.1 The Analytics Continuum

In healthcare, we primarily deal with four stages of analytics. At the intermediate level, we focus on the first two:

  • Descriptive Analytics ("What happened?"): This is the foundation. It involves summarizing historical data. For example, looking at a DHIS2 dashboard and seeing that 150 cases of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) were recorded in the province last month.
  • Diagnostic Analytics ("Why did it happen?"): This involves drilling down into the data to find correlations and root causes. You don't just accept the 150 SAM cases; you cross-reference the data by district, age group, and correlate it with recent drought mapping or agricultural output data to find the localized cause.

Module 2: Navigating DHIS2 Analytical Tools

Zimbabwe relies heavily on DHIS2. To find diagnostic answers, you must master its three core analytical applications:

2.1 Dashboards, Pivot Tables, and Data Visualizer

  • Dashboards: Your daily command center. A dashboard gives you a high-level visual summary of key performance indicators (KPIs) like Maternal Mortality Ratio or ARV stockout rates.
  • Data Visualizer: Converts raw data into charts (bar, line, pie). This is used for presentations and quick trend spotting.
  • Pivot Tables: The most powerful tool for an intermediate analyst. A pivot table allows you to dynamically cross-tabulate data. You can take a massive dataset of "All OPD visits" and pivot it to show "Malaria Cases" (Rows) by "District" (Columns) filtered by "Children Under 5" (Filter).

Module 3: Calculating Epidemic Thresholds

3.1 Endemic vs. Epidemic

In many African regions, diseases like Malaria are endemic—they are always present at a baseline level. An epidemic occurs when cases rise significantly above that normal baseline. But how do we define "significantly above"? We use Epidemic Thresholds.

3.2 Action Lines and Moving Averages

To detect an outbreak, data analysts calculate an Action Line (or Epidemic Threshold) based on historical data. A common method is taking the average number of weekly cases for the past 5 non-epidemic years, and adding two standard deviations.

If the current week's case count on your DHIS2 line graph crosses above this Action Line, it triggers an immediate public health response (e.g., deploying mobile testing units and bed nets), rather than waiting for hospitals to overflow.

Module 4: Data Quality Assurance (DQA)

4.1 Spotting Outliers

Before you analyze data, you must clean it. A critical intermediate skill is identifying Outliers—data points that differ wildly from the norm. If a rural clinic that normally reports 10 births a month suddenly reports 1,000 births, that is an outlier. It is almost certainly a data entry typo (an extra two zeros), not a sudden population boom.

4.2 Routine Data Quality Assessment (RDQA)

Health ministries mandate regular RDQAs. This involves a physical audit where an analyst visits a facility, takes the digital DHIS2 report, and manually recounts the physical paper ward registers for that month to check for discrepancies (Over-reporting or Under-reporting). High-quality analysis is impossible without rigorous DQA.

Module 5: Root Cause Analysis (RCA) in Clinical Data

5.1 The "5 Whys" Framework

When data reveals a critical failure—like a sudden spike in neonatal mortality or an ARV stockout—analysts use RCA tools to find the core issue. The simplest is the "5 Whys" technique. Ask "Why" iteratively until you hit the systemic root cause, rather than just the surface symptom.

5.2 The Ishikawa (Fishbone) Diagram

For complex health data problems, we use the Fishbone diagram to categorize potential causes of a data trend. Categories typically include: People (Staffing), Processes (Protocols), Equipment (Machinery/IT), Materials (Drugs/Supplies), and Environment. This prevents hospital management from just blaming the nurses when the data shows a failure was actually caused by a broken supply chain process.

Module 6: Data Storytelling & Visualization

6.1 Choosing the Right Chart

Presenting data to a Provincial Medical Director requires clarity. If you use the wrong chart, your actionable intelligence is lost.

  • Line Graphs: Best for showing Trends over Time (e.g., Malaria cases from January to December).
  • Bar Charts: Best for showing Comparisons between categories (e.g., Comparing bed occupancy rates across five different district hospitals).
  • Pie Charts: Best for showing Parts of a Whole (e.g., Percentage breakdown of funding sources). Use sparingly in health data, as humans are bad at estimating angles.

6.2 The Contextual Narrative

A chart without a narrative is just a picture. A data analyst must always provide the "So What?" (e.g., "This line graph shows a 40% spike in diarrheal diseases in District A. Therefore, we must immediately dispatch water purification tablets to that specific region.")

Module 7: Data Governance & Ethical Reporting

7.1 Zimbabwe Cyber and Data Protection Act (2021)

As you transition into data analysis, you will handle massive datasets. Under Zimbabwean law, health data is highly sensitive. You must ensure that any dataset exported from DHIS2 or OpenMRS for analysis is completely De-identified (Anonymized). Never share spreadsheets containing patient names, ID numbers, or exact addresses via insecure channels like WhatsApp.

The Analyst's Oath

Data is not just numbers on a screen; it represents human lives, suffering, and outcomes. Ethical data analysis means protecting privacy, never manipulating axes on a chart to hide failures, and always seeking the truth to improve clinical care.
